# The interface is not friendly, and here is where

**Date:** 2026-08-25
**Source:** the owner using the app against a real site (ritecoach), plus my own
read of the screens as built.

The verdict, in the owner's words: *"the UI is kinda confusing… even I didn't
understand some stuff"* — from the person who specified it. That is the strongest
possible signal, because every excuse available for a new user ("they'll learn
the vocabulary", "specialists expect density") does not apply to him.

**This is a positioning change, not a polish task.** PRODUCT.md's Users section
said density over hand-holding, aimed at technical SEO specialists who want no
onboarding. The audience is widening toward marketing agencies, where the person
opening a crawl may not read status codes fluently. PRODUCT.md § Users is
amended accordingly; this file is the specific evidence behind that amendment.

## What is actually wrong

Ordered by how badly it fails someone who is not the person who wrote it.

1. **Issue chips show rule identifiers, not findings.**
   `indexability.canonical-elsewhere · 6,000` is a database key. Every rule
   already carries a human `description` and a `remediation` in the registry and
   the interface shows neither. This is the worst offender and the cheapest fix.

2. **The header shows engineering diagnostics.**
   `engine 0.0.1 · schema 13 · 30 rules` is a version check written for the
   developer. Nobody opening a crawl needs a schema number.

3. **There are no screens.** The new-crawl form, the open bar and the results
   grid are stacked on one page, so the form occupies a third of the window
   while results are being read. Setup, running and results are three states of
   one task and should look like it.

4. **Engine vocabulary leaks into labels.** "Admitted" is a lifecycle term.
   "Queued 0 waiting" reads as jargon. `2xx`/`4xx` are correct for a specialist
   and meaningless to an account manager — the count needs a word beside it.

5. **No empty state.** First launch is a blank window with form fields and no
   indication of what the app is for or what to do first.

6. **No feedback on what a setting does.** "Per-host requests: 4, max 16" and
   "Delay per request" are the two controls that decide whether a crawl is
   polite or gets the user blocked, with nothing saying so. The owner ran a
   default crawl at 7 URL/s against a site with a 60 req/min limit — the
   interface gave him no reason to think that would happen.

7. **Errors are engine strings.** "output already exists: /tmp/x.pounce" is
   accurate and unhelpful; it should offer a new name.

## What is *not* wrong, and must not be "fixed"

Density itself. A results grid that shows a lot at once, mono URLs, and numeric
status codes are correct for the primary user and for anyone doing this work
repeatedly. The problem is rawness — identifiers where sentences belong,
diagnostics where product information belongs — not information density.

## The layout to aim at

The owner's reference is **Screaming Frog's layout, not its components** — the
Swing widgets are what reads as dated; the arrangement is why the app is usable
at density:

- **Results appear while the crawl runs.** This is the biggest single gap, and
  it is nearly free here: the store is disk-backed from the first row and a WAL
  reader already works under a live writer (tested in T3.3). The app simply does
  not open the file until the crawl finishes. On a 100k-page site nobody should
  wait twenty minutes to learn that page 200 has two `<h1>`s.
- **A left rail of issue categories with live counts**, which is both the
  navigation and the summary. It answers "what is wrong with this site" without
  reading a table.
- **Tabs across the top** for views of the same crawl.
- **A detail pane** for the selected row, rather than a separate screen.

What must *not* be copied: the components. The direction is friendly and
modern — this is for marketing agencies, not for developers reading a debugger.

## Where this goes

New tasks under M4 rather than a rewrite. Items 1, 2, 4 and 7 are wording and
small components. Items 3 and 5 are layout. Item 6 is a real design question:
how does an interface make politeness legible without lecturing?
